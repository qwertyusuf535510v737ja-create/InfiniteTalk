### COLD OPEN (0:00 – 0:40)

**[EKRAN: qora fon, oq matn bilan bitta raqam paydo bo'ladi: 3.3 kPa]**

In May 2007, a team of doctors climbed Mount Everest carrying syringes.

At 8,400 metres, on a ledge called the Balcony, four of them lay down in the
snow, took off a glove, and drew arterial blood from their own femoral arteries.

**[PAUZA]**

Then they carried those samples down to a laboratory at Base Camp before the
blood could spoil.

**[EKRAN: laboratoriya natijalari jadvali, PaO₂ ustuni ajratilgan]**

The results were published in the New England Journal of Medicine. The partial
pressure of oxygen in their arterial blood had fallen to around three point
three kilopascals. Oxygen saturation, around fifty-five percent.

If you walked into an emergency department at sea level with those numbers,
you would not be asked questions. You would be intubated.

These men were conscious. Two of them were about to climb higher.

**[EKRAN: sarlavha kartasi — BARE FACTS · THE DEATH ZONE]**

This is what the death zone actually does to a human body. Not the story
version. The measured version.

### ACT 1 — The death zone is not a place (0:40 – 3:30)

**[EKRAN: Yer atmosferasining bosim grafigi, balandlik bo'yicha]**

First, a correction. The death zone is not a line drawn on a mountain. There is
no rope, no sign, no change in the rock.

The number usually given is eight thousand metres. That number is a convention,
not a measurement. What it marks is the altitude at which one specific biological
process stops working.

That process is acclimatisation.

**[EKRAN: oddiy diagramma — atmosfera bosimi 8 000 metrda dengiz sathining ~35%]**

At sea level, the air is twenty-one percent oxygen. At the summit of Everest,
the air is still twenty-one percent oxygen. That surprises people. The
composition does not change.

What changes is pressure. At eight thousand metres, atmospheric pressure is
roughly a third of what it is at sea level. The same percentage of oxygen,
pushed by a third of the force.

**[PAUZA]**

Your lungs do not measure percentages. They measure pressure. And below a
certain pressure, oxygen simply stops crossing from the air into your blood
fast enough to replace what you are burning.

**[EKRAN: alveola diagrammasi, gazlar almashinuvi]**

Between sea level and about five thousand five hundred metres, your body can
compensate. It makes more red blood cells. It changes the pH of your blood. It
rebuilds capillary density in muscle. Given weeks, it adapts.

Above roughly eight thousand metres, that compensation runs out. Every system
that can be tuned has been tuned to its limit, and the deficit is still there.

From that altitude upward, you are not adapting. You are spending.

The commonly quoted survivable window is sixteen to twenty hours. Treat that
as a rule of thumb rather than a constant — it varies with the individual, the
weather, and how long they were already high before entering it. What does not
vary is the direction. From the moment you enter, the clock only runs one way.

### ACT 2 — The fast failures (3:30 – 8:30)

**[EKRAN: bo'lim kartasi — 1. THE LUNGS]**

Two conditions kill people in the death zone within hours. Both are forms of
fluid going where fluid should not be.

The first is high-altitude pulmonary edema. HAPE.

**[EKRAN: o'pka kapillyarlari diagrammasi]**

In low oxygen, the blood vessels in your lungs do something that makes sense
almost everywhere else in the body, and is catastrophic here. They constrict.

At sea level, when one part of your lung is poorly ventilated, narrowing the
vessels there redirects blood to a better-supplied part. It is an efficiency
mechanism.

At altitude, the entire lung is poorly oxygenated. So the entire pulmonary
vascular bed constricts at once. Pressure in the pulmonary artery climbs. The
capillary walls begin to leak.

**[PAUZA]**

Fluid enters the air sacs. The surface where oxygen is supposed to cross is now
underwater.

**[EKRAN: simptomlar ro'yxati, ketma-ket paydo bo'ladi]**

The presentation is quiet at first, and that is the danger. A dry cough. Getting
tired faster than yesterday. Breathlessness that does not settle when you stop
walking.

Then the cough produces something. Pink. Frothy. In the worst cases, frank
blood.

By the time a climber is coughing pink foam, they are drowning in fluid their
own circulation produced. HAPE typically appears two to five days after arriving
at altitude.

**[EKRAN: bo'lim kartasi — 2. THE BRAIN]**

The second condition is high-altitude cerebral edema. HACE. It is rarer and it
is worse.

The same leakage, in the one organ that has no room to swell.

**[EKRAN: bosh suyagi kesimi, miya shishi]**

Hypoxia changes the permeability of cerebral vessels. Fluid crosses into brain
tissue. The brain swells inside a rigid box. Intracranial pressure rises.

What it looks like from the outside is a person becoming stupid.

Headache first. Then confusion. Then drowsiness that looks like ordinary
exhaustion — and at altitude, everyone is exhausted, so it gets explained away.

**[PAUZA]**

The reliable early sign is not mental at all. It is ataxia. Loss of coordinated
walking.

**[EKRAN: to'g'ri chiziqda yurish testi]**

This is why experienced climbers make each other walk a straight line, heel to
toe. Not to check if someone is tired. To check whether the part of the brain
that manages balance is still receiving enough oxygen to do it.

A climber who cannot walk that line has hours. Untreated, HACE progresses to
stupor, then coma, then death.

**[EKRAN: matn — THE ONLY TREATMENT IS DESCENT]**

And here is the part that makes the death zone different from every other
medical emergency.

There is no drug that fixes this. Dexamethasone buys time. Bottled oxygen buys
time. A portable hyperbaric bag buys time. None of them treat the cause, because
the cause is the altitude itself.

The only treatment is to go down. And going down, from eight thousand metres,
with a brain that can no longer coordinate walking, is a problem with no good
solution.

A helicopter cannot reach you — the air is too thin to generate lift at that
altitude for a loaded rescue. Carrying an unconscious adult down technical
ground, while every rescuer is also hypoxic, is close to impossible.

This is the honest reason bodies remain on that mountain. Not indifference.
Physics.

### ACT 3 — The slow failure (8:30 – 12:30)

**[EKRAN: bo'lim kartasi — 3. THE SLOW ONE]**

HAPE and HACE are the fast failures. There is a slower one, and it starts long
before the summit day.

**[EKRAN: Operation Everest II arxiv fotosi yoki barokamera diagrammasi]**

In 1985, a study called Operation Everest II put eight men in a decompression
chamber and lowered the pressure over forty days until it matched the summit of
Everest. No mountain. No cold. No avalanche risk. Only the pressure.

They measured what the lack of oxygen alone does to a human body.

**[EKRAN: raqamlar ketma-ket — 9% · 25% · 28%]**

Nine percent of body mass, gone.

Muscle biopsies showed the cross-sectional area of both slow and fast twitch
fibres reduced by about twenty-five percent.

And maximum oxygen uptake — the ceiling on how much work the body can do — fell
to roughly twenty-eight percent of each man's sea-level value.

**[PAUZA]**

Read that again. At summit-equivalent pressure, a fit adult has a little over a
quarter of their normal aerobic capacity.

**[EKRAN: oddiy tushuntirish — appetite suppression + malabsorption + catabolism]**

Three things drive the wasting. Appetite collapses at altitude, so intake drops.
Absorption becomes less efficient. And the body, short of oxygen and short of
fuel, begins breaking down its own tissue to supply it.

That is where the phrase "the body consumes itself" comes from. It is not
poetry. It is a measurable loss of muscle fibre area.

**[EKRAN: vaqt o'qi — ekspeditsiya haftalarini ko'rsatadi]**

And notice the timescale, because it is the part that usually gets told wrong.
This wasting is not something that happens in the death zone in a single day.
It accumulates across the whole expedition — weeks at altitude before summit
day.

Which produces the real trap. A climber arrives at the base of the final push
already weaker than when they arrived at the mountain, then attempts the hardest
physical effort of the trip, on roughly a quarter of their aerobic capacity, in
the only altitude band where the body can no longer recover.

Every part of that sentence is working against the person inside it.

### ACT 4 — What the numbers actually mean (12:30 – 15:00)

**[EKRAN: 2007-yilgi o'lchov raqamlariga qaytish]**

Back to those blood samples.

At sea level, a healthy adult has an arterial oxygen partial pressure of roughly
thirteen kilopascals, and a saturation around ninety-eight percent.

**[EKRAN: yonma-yon solishtirish — 13 kPa / 98% · 3.3 kPa / 55%]**

On the Balcony of Everest, those four doctors measured about three point three
kilopascals, and about fifty-five percent.

Those are not values a body is built to hold. They are values a body is
temporarily surviving, while every process that depends on oxygen runs at a
deficit, and the debt is paid out of tissue.

**[PAUZA]**

What makes that measurement remarkable is not the number itself. It is that a
human being, at that number, was able to lie down in the snow, locate a femoral
artery, draw blood correctly, seal the sample, stand up, and keep moving.

**[EKRAN: yakuniy matn kartasi]**

The death zone does not kill people by being cold, or steep, or far from help,
although it is all three. It kills them by removing the one input that every
repair process in the body requires, and then starting a clock.

Everything above eight thousand metres — the exhaustion, the confusion, the
drowning lungs, the wasting muscle — is one deficiency, expressed in four
different organs.
